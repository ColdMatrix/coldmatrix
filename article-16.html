QR Codes Explained — How They Work and Where They're Actually Useful

Published by ColdMatrix • 5 min read

QR codes went from a niche manufacturing tool to something almost everyone scans daily. Here's what's actually happening when you scan one, and where they genuinely add value for a business.

How QR Codes Actually Work

A QR (Quick Response) code encodes data — usually a URL, text, or contact information — into a grid of black and white squares. Your phone's camera reads the pattern, decodes it back into data, and takes action (usually opening a link) — all without needing an internet connection to read the code itself.

What Can Be Encoded

  • Website links (most common use)
  • Plain text or messages
  • WiFi network credentials (scan to auto-connect)
  • Contact details (vCard format — scan to save a contact directly)
  • Payment information

Business Use Cases Beyond the Obvious

  • Business cards — a QR code that saves your contact info directly to someone's phone, no typing required
  • Product packaging — linking to instructions, warranty registration, or authenticity verification
  • Event signage — quick registration or feedback forms without anyone typing a URL
  • Physical-to-digital bridges — printed materials linking to videos, reviews, or updated pricing

Do QR Codes Expire?

A static QR code (like the ones generated here) never expires — it directly encodes the data, so as long as the underlying link or content stays valid, the code works indefinitely.

A Common Mistake

Making the code too small for reliable scanning, or placing it somewhere with poor lighting or awkward camera angles. As a rule of thumb, a QR code should be at least 2×2 cm when printed, with a plain, high-contrast background.
