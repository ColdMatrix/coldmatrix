📚 Percentage Calculation: The Complete Reference Guide

Percentages are everywhere — from store discounts and taxes to profit margins and test scores. Understanding how to calculate them accurately saves time and prevents costly mistakes. This guide explains the three essential percentage operations you'll encounter daily, plus real-world examples you can use immediately.

1. Finding a Percentage of a Number

Formula: Result = (Percentage ÷ 100) × Total

Example: What is 15% of 400,000?

(15 ÷ 100) × 400,000 = 60,000

This is the most common calculation — used for discounts, tips, and sales tax. Whenever you see "X% off" or "X% of the total", this is the mode you need.

2. Reverse Percentage (What Percent One Number Is of Another)

Formula: Percentage = (Part ÷ Total) × 100

Example: 150,000 is what percent of 700,000?

(150,000 ÷ 700,000) × 100 = 21.43%

Use this to understand proportions — e.g., what portion of your budget is spent on rent, or what percentage of students passed an exam.

3. Percentage Increase or Decrease

Formula: Percentage Change = ((New Value − Old Value) ÷ Old Value) × 100

Example: Sales increased from 500 to 650 units.

((650 − 500) ÷ 500) × 100 = 30% increase

Essential for tracking growth, inflation, and performance. A positive result means increase; negative means decrease.

5. Where Percentage Calculations Are Used

  • Business & Finance: Profit margins, discounts, taxes, and commission calculations.
  • Education: Grades, test scores, and statistical analysis.
  • Shopping: Sale prices and discount comparisons.
  • Health & Fitness: Body fat percentage and progress tracking.
  • Investment: Return on investment and growth rates.
  • Salary & HR: Increments, bonuses, and salary revisions.

6. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing margin and markup: Margin is profit ÷ revenue, markup is profit ÷ cost. They're very different numbers.
  • Adding percentages: 10% + 10% is not 20% — it's 21% when compounding.
  • Reverse percentage confusion: A 20% discount followed by 20% price increase does not return to the original price.
  • Using wrong base: Always know whether you're calculating from cost or selling price.

7. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I calculate 20% of 1,500?
A: (20 ÷ 100) × 1,500 = 300.

Q: A 15% discount on PKR 3,000?
A: You save 450, final price 2,550.

Q: How do I calculate percentage change?
A: (New − Old) ÷ Old × 100. If positive, it's increase; negative, decrease.

Q: What is reverse percentage?
A: Finding what percent one number is of another. Example: 150 is what percent of 700? Answer: 21.43%.

Q: Are percentages the same as fractions?
A: Yes, 25% = 25/100 = 0.25 = 1/4.

Q: Can percentages be negative?
A: Yes — in percentage change, a negative result means a decrease from the old value.
