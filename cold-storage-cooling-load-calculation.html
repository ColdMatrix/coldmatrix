How to Calculate Cooling Load for a Cold Storage Room

By Waseem Akhtar
Founder & CEO, Cold Matrix Advanced Solutions
Published: November 2026

If you undersize a refrigeration system, your product will spoil during peak summer. If you oversize it, you will waste thousands of rupees on unnecessary electricity and equipment costs. Accurate cooling load calculation is the most critical step in cold storage engineering.

Here is the step-by-step engineering methodology we use at Cold Matrix Advanced Solutions to determine the exact cooling capacity (in BTU/hr or Tons of Refrigeration) required for a facility.

1. Transmission Load (Heat through the panels)

This is the heat transferring from the hot outside environment into the cold room through the walls, ceiling, and floor. To calculate this, you need the surface area of the room, the thickness of your PU/PIR sandwich panels, and the temperature difference (Delta T) between the ambient outdoor temperature and the target indoor temperature.

Pro Tip: In cities like Karachi, we always calculate this using a peak summer ambient temperature of 45°C to 48°C to ensure the system can handle the worst-case scenario.

2. Product Load (Heat from the goods)

The product load is the heat that must be removed from the items being stored. This is usually the largest load and requires calculating different types of heat:

  • Sensible Heat: The heat removed to lower the product's temperature from the harvest/processing temperature down to the storage temperature.
  • Latent Heat: If you are freezing products (like meat or poultry), you must calculate the massive amount of energy required to change the product from a liquid state to a frozen state.
  • Respiration Heat: If you are storing fresh fruits and vegetables, they are still "alive" and generate heat as they breathe. This must be factored into your calculation.

3. Infiltration Load (Heat from opening doors)

Every time a worker opens the cold room door to load or unload goods, hot outside air rushes in, bringing both heat and moisture. We calculate this using the room volume and the estimated number of "air changes per day."

Engineering Tip: Installing heavy-duty PVC strip curtains or fast-acting sliding doors drastically reduces this load and saves power.

4. Internal Loads (Equipment and People)

Heat is generated from inside the room by:

  • Lighting: LED lights are recommended, but they still generate some heat.
  • Forklifts: Electric forklifts or manual trolleys moving inside.
  • People: The workers inside the room generate body heat (usually calculated based on the number of workers and hours spent inside).

5. The Safety Factor

Once you have added up all the above loads, you must add a 10% to 15% Safety Factor. This accounts for:

  • Days when the ambient temperature is higher than expected.
  • Dirt or dust on the condenser coils reducing efficiency.
  • Equipment aging over time.

Only after adding this safety factor do you select your condensing units and evaporators.
