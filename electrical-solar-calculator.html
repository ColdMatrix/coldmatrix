📖 Complete Guide to Electrical Load & Solar System Calculation

This calculator helps you understand and plan your electrical energy needs — from the appliances you use daily to the solar panels, batteries, and inverter required to power them. Whether you're a homeowner planning a backup system, a business owner calculating energy costs, or an engineer doing preliminary design, this tool simplifies the entire process.

What Is Electrical Load?

Electrical load is the total amount of power consumed by all your electrical appliances and devices. It's measured in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW). Understanding your load is the first step in designing any electrical or solar system because it tells you how much energy you need to generate, store, and supply.

Connected Load vs Actual Consumption

Connected Load: The total wattage of all appliances if they were turned on at the same time. This number is usually higher than actual usage because you rarely run everything simultaneously.

Actual Consumption: The real energy used based on how many hours each appliance runs. This is what matters for sizing solar panels and batteries.

Example: You may have 10 lights of 20W each (connected load = 200W), but if you only use 5 lights for 5 hours, actual consumption is 5 × 20 × 5 = 500Wh (0.5 kWh).

How Daily Energy Consumption Is Calculated

Daily energy consumption = Total wattage × Hours used per day

For each appliance: Quantity × Wattage × Hours = Watt-hours (Wh)

Sum all appliances to get total daily Wh, then divide by 1000 to get kWh.

Example: A 200W refrigerator running 24 hours = 200 × 24 = 4800Wh = 4.8 kWh per day.

What Is Solar Panel Sizing?

Solar panel sizing determines how many panels you need to generate your daily energy requirement. It depends on:

  • Peak Sun Hours: Average hours of full sunlight per day (usually 4–6 hours in most regions).
  • System Efficiency: Losses from wiring, inverter, temperature, and panel degradation (typically 75–85% efficient).
  • Desired Solar Share: What percentage of your energy should come from solar (50%, 75%, 100%).
  • Panel Wattage: Common sizes are 400W, 450W, 500W, 550W, and 600W+.

Formula: Required Solar Capacity (kW) = Daily Consumption (kWh) ÷ (Peak Sun Hours × System Efficiency)

Understanding Battery Backup

Batteries store excess solar energy for use at night or during power outages. Battery sizing depends on:

  • Backup Hours: How many hours you need power when solar is not available.
  • Battery Voltage: Common systems use 12V, 24V, or 48V.
  • Battery Capacity: Measured in Amp-hours (Ah).
  • Depth of Discharge (DoD): How much of the battery can be used safely. Lead acid = 50%, Lithium = 80–90%.
  • System Efficiency: Energy lost during charge/discharge.

Formula: Battery Energy (Wh) = (Load × Backup Hours) ÷ (DoD × Efficiency)

How Inverter Sizing Works

The inverter converts DC power from solar panels and batteries into AC power for your appliances. It must be sized to handle your maximum load plus surge requirements.

Formula: Inverter Size = Maximum Load × (1 + Surge%) × (1 + Safety Margin%)

Surge accounts for appliances that draw extra power when starting (motors, compressors, pumps). Safety margin adds buffer for future expansion.

Basic Cable Sizing Explained

Cables carry current from solar panels to batteries, and from inverter to appliances. Proper cable sizing prevents overheating and voltage drop.

Cable size depends on:

  • Load Current: Amperes flowing through the cable.
  • Voltage: System voltage (12V, 24V, 48V, 220V).
  • Cable Length: Distance between components.
  • Conductor Material: Copper or Aluminum.
  • Allowable Voltage Drop: Usually 2–3% for solar systems.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Underestimating consumption: Always add 20–25% margin for future needs.
  • Wrong battery type: Lead acid and lithium have different depth of discharge limits.
  • Ignoring peak sun hours: Solar generation varies by region and season.
  • Undersized cables: Can cause overheating, fire risk, and voltage loss.
  • Not considering surge requirements: Motors and pumps need extra starting power.

Real-World Example

Suppose a home has:

  • 10 LED lights × 15W × 6 hours = 900Wh
  • 3 fans × 75W × 10 hours = 2250Wh
  • 1 refrigerator × 200W × 24 hours = 4800Wh
  • 1 TV × 100W × 4 hours = 400Wh

Total daily consumption = 8350Wh = 8.35 kWh

With 5 peak sun hours and 80% efficiency, required solar capacity = 8.35 ÷ (5 × 0.8) = 2.09 kW

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I run my entire house on solar?
A: Yes, if your solar capacity and battery storage match your daily consumption and backup needs.

Q: How many solar panels do I need for a home?
A: It depends on consumption. An average home using 10 kWh/day might need 8–12 panels of 550W each.

Q: What battery type is best?
A: Lithium-ion is best for most solar systems due to higher efficiency, longer life, and deeper discharge. Lead acid is cheaper but requires more maintenance.

Q: Does this calculator work for three-phase systems?
A: Yes, it supports single-phase and three-phase cable sizing.

Q: Is this tool accurate for final installation?
A: No, it provides preliminary estimates. Always have a qualified electrical engineer verify the final design and local code compliance.
